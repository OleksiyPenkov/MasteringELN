Part 3 · Chapter 10

Principles of Experiment Planning and the Taguchi Method

In This Chapter

  • Seeing why unplanned trial-and-error runs waste laboratory time and samples
  • Connecting a testable scientific hypothesis to a structured experiment series
  • Choosing between one-factor sweeps, full factorial grids, and Taguchi orthogonal arrays
  • Using level means, Delta effect sizes, and factor ranks to find dominant parameters

In Parts 1 and 2 (Chapter 4 through Chapter 9), you learned how to record a single specimen tree so every stored number traces back to its raw instrument file. Real research rarely stops at one sample. You vary magnetron power, substrate bias, gas pressure, or annealing temperature across a series of depositions to find which parameter controls film thickness, hardness, wear rate, or multilayer period. This chapter explains why you should plan those runs before going to the chamber and how APS ELN uses sweeps, full grids, and Taguchi orthogonal arrays (Figure 10-1).

Why unplanned experiments fail in the laboratory

When students start a new coating or treatment study without a written plan, they usually fall into four laboratory traps:

  1. Changing two parameters at once: Suppose you raise substrate bias from 50 V50\,\text{V}50V to 100 V100\,\text{V}100V and also lower argon pressure from 0.4 Pa0.4\,\text{Pa}0.4Pa to 0.2 Pa0.2\,\text{Pa}0.2Pa between two runs. You then cannot tell which change increased the film hardness.
  2. Unrecorded baseline drift: Fixed conditions such as target-to-substrate distance, base pressure, or substrate cleaning time are easy to omit from a notebook. Without them, day-to-day drift can look like a real physical effect.
  3. Orphan runs: When you sit down to write your thesis or paper, a project folder full of unlinked Deposition procedures does not show which question runs 12 through 17 tested or why run 14 was stopped.
  4. Combinatorial explosion: A magnetron sputtering process often has four to seven adjustable knobs (power, bias voltage, working pressure, reactive gas flow, substrate temperature, and layer times). Testing 4 factors at 3 levels each by brute force requires 34=813^4 = 8134=81 depositions and 81 sets of characterization scans.

APS ELN prevents these traps with two linked planning windows under the Project menu:

  • The Hypothesis Tree (Project > Hypotheses, Chapter 11): records what you expect to happen and why (Statement) and what changed from the parent idea (Change). It then links the experimental Runs, the cited Evidence, and whether the expectation was Supported, Refuted, or Inconclusive.
  • The Experiment Series (Project > Series..., Chapter 12): generates the matrix of planned points and writes target setpoints onto each new procedure as planned values. It also tracks which runs are deposited or measured and plots the results across the series.

In ELN

Both Project > Hypotheses and Project > Series… open in their own separate windows with their own Windows taskbar buttons. They stay open while you work in the main window, so you can keep your hypothesis or series table side by side with your item tree.

Factors, levels, fixed conditions, and responses

Every structured experiment series in APS ELN is built from four parts:

  • Factors (1 to 7 varied parameters): the experimental inputs (Condition value types) you step across the series, such as Power, Bias, Pressure, or deposition Time. When two factors share the same value type (such as the deposition times of two layers), give each factor a Label such as Mo layer and Si layer. APS ELN displays the label in brackets after the value type (Time (Mo layer), s).
  • Levels: the specific numeric setpoints you test for each factor, such as 100; 200; 300 for Power, W.
  • Fixed conditions: the input parameters (Condition value types) held constant across every run in the series, such as Pressure =0.267 Pa= 0.267\,\text{Pa}=0.267Pa (2 mTorr2\,\text{mTorr}2mTorr) or Cycles =20= 20=20, with an optional tolerance.
  • Responses: the measured outputs (Result value types) extracted on every point, such as step-height Thickness (ddd), Hardness (HITH_{\text{IT}}HIT​), Wear rate (KKK), or multilayer Period (DDD).

Remember

In APS ELN, a factor or fixed condition is a Condition (EXP_CONDITION on the Procedure). A response is a Result (TEST_RESULT on the procedure or its child Actions). Separating conditions from results during value extraction (Chapter 9) is what allows the series table to assemble itself automatically.

Sweeps, full grids, and Taguchi orthogonal arrays

Before you cut substrates or pump down the chamber, choose the point-generation method that matches the number of factors you are testing (Figure 10-1 and Table 10-1).

1. One-factor sweep (1 factor)

In a one-factor sweep, you vary a single factor across several levels (for example, sputtering power at 100 W100\,\text{W}100W, 200 W200\,\text{W}200W, and 300 W300\,\text{W}300W) while holding every other condition fixed. Use a one-factor sweep when you are calibrating a deposition rate, checking thickness linearity against time, or testing a single physical variable.

2. Full factorial grid (Full grid, 1 to 3 factors, up to 27 points)

Two or three parameters often interact, such as magnetron Power and substrate Bias during BN/DLC\text{BN/DLC}BN/DLC deposition. Varying one factor at a time can then miss the best corner of the parameter window. Choosing Full grid generates every combination of your factor levels:

  • Two factors with 3 levels each produce 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9 plan points.
  • Three factors with 3 levels each produce 3×3×3=273 \times 3 \times 3 = 273×3×3=27 plan points.

A full grid grows exponentially as mkm^kmk, where kkk is the number of factors and mmm is the number of levels. Four factors at 3 levels would require 34=813^4 = 8134=81 depositions. APS ELN therefore limits Full grid to at most 3 factors and 27 points. When you add a third factor in the plan dialog, APS ELN switches Point generation to Taguchi orthogonal array automatically.

3. Taguchi orthogonal arrays (Taguchi orthogonal array, 2 to 7 factors)

Developed by Genichi Taguchi for engineering process optimization, an orthogonal array denoted LNL_NLN​ is a fractional factorial table with NNN rows (depositions) and kkk columns (factors). APS ELN includes six standard orthogonal arrays covering 2 to 7 factors (Table 10-1).

What makes an array like L9 orthogonal is its two-way balance across all columns (Table 10-2):

  1. Equal level frequency: In the 9 rows of L9, each of the three levels (1, 2, 3) of every factor appears exactly 3 times.
  2. Equal pair frequency: Take any two factors in Table 10-2, such as Power (Factor A) and Bias (Factor B). Every possible ordered pair of levels ((Low, Low), (Low, Med), (Low, High), (Med, Low), …, (High, High)) appears exactly once among the 9 runs.

Look at Points 1, 2, and 3 in Table 10-2, where Power is held at Level 1 (100 W100\,\text{W}100W). Across those three runs, Bias visits Levels 1, 2, and 3; Pressure visits Levels 1, 2, and 3; and N2 flow visits Levels 1, 2, and 3. The same balance holds for the three runs at 200 W200\,\text{W}200W (Points 4–6) and the three runs at 300 W300\,\text{W}300W (Points 7–9). When you compare the average hardness at 100 W100\,\text{W}100W, 200 W200\,\text{W}200W, and 300 W300\,\text{W}300W, the main effects of the other three factors cancel out cleanly.

Tip

Suppose one factor has 2 levels (such as substrate heater Off vs. On), while up to 6 other factors have 3 levels each. APS ELN selects the mixed L18 array automatically. You can put the 2-level factor at any position in your factor list.

Reading main effects: level means, Delta, and factor rank

Once you run the depositions in an orthogonal array and extract the response values, open the Plot tab of Project > Series… (Chapter 12). Tick Level means (Taguchi) to evaluate each factor.

For a chosen plan factor XXX and response YYY, APS ELN calculates three quantities:

  1. The level mean (YˉX=L\bar{Y}_{X=L}YˉX=L​): For each planned level LLL of factor XXX, ELN averages the confirmed response values Y1,…,YnLY_1, \dots, Y_{n_L}Y1​,…,YnL​​ recorded at that level:
YˉX=L=1nL∑i=1nLYi\bar{Y}_{X=L} = \frac{1}{n_L} \sum_{i=1}^{n_L} Y_iYˉX=L​=nL​1​i=1∑nL​​Yi​
  1. The level spread (error bar sLs_LsL​): When nL≥2n_L \ge 2nL​≥2 runs were measured at level LLL, the vertical error bar on the plot is the sample standard deviation across those runs:
sL=1nL−1∑i=1nL(Yi−YˉX=L)2s_L = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_L - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n_L} \left(Y_i - \bar{Y}_{X=L}\right)^2}sL​=nL​−11​i=1∑nL​​(Yi​−YˉX=L​)2​

When only nL=1n_L = 1nL​=1 run has been measured at level LLL, the error bar shows that single value’s stored tolerance.

  1. The effect size (ΔX\Delta_XΔX​) and factor rank: Below the result list on the Plot tab, APS ELN displays Delta (ΔX\Delta_XΔX​). It equals the largest level mean minus the smallest level mean of factor XXX:
ΔX=max⁡L(YˉX=L)−min⁡L(YˉX=L)\Delta_X = \max_{L}\left(\bar{Y}_{X=L}\right) - \min_{L}\left(\bar{Y}_{X=L}\right)ΔX​=Lmax​(YˉX=L​)−Lmin​(YˉX=L​)

Alongside ΔX\Delta_XΔX​, APS ELN shows the factor’s rank among all kkk factors in the plan, such as Delta = 10 nm (rank 1 of 3) or Delta = 2.4 GPa (rank 1 of 4). Rank 1 is the factor with the largest Δ\DeltaΔ: the parameter that produces the largest change in the measured response across its tested levels. Factors with equal Δ\DeltaΔ share the same rank.

Warning: deviated setpoints and unconfirmed planned values are excluded from level means

Orthogonal cancellation requires each run to sit at its planned factor level within tolerance. When a deposition’s stored factor value differs from its planned level beyond tolerance, APS ELN excludes that run from the factor’s level means. The plot note then displays n point(s) differ from the plan and are left out of level means. Unconfirmed grey italic planned values are also excluded until you confirm them.
Going Deeper: when Taguchi screening is enough and when to run a follow-up Full Grid

Taguchi level-means analysis is a main-effects screening method. It assumes that the direct effect of each factor is larger than two-factor interactions (where the effect of Factor A reverses direction depending on Factor B). In addition, APS ELN’s Level means (Taguchi) plot computes direct response means and Δ\DeltaΔ ranks rather than logarithmic signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios or an analysis of variance (ANOVA).

This makes a two-stage workflow very effective in practice:

  1. Stage 1 (Screening with Taguchi L9 or L18): Propose your initial screening hypothesis (Chapter 11) and run a 9-point L9 series across 4 candidate parameters (Chapter 12). Use Level means (Taguchi) to identify the Rank 1 and Rank 2 factors, and fix the low-impact Rank 3 and Rank 4 factors at their best levels.
  2. Stage 2 (Mapping with a 2-factor Full Grid): Propose a New follow-up… hypothesis and create a 2-factor Full grid series over just the Rank 1 and Rank 2 factors. You can also export the table to OriginLab via Copy table or Save CSV… to map their response surface in detail.
